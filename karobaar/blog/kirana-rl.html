Teaching a small model to run a kirana shop

Notes from our first month of reinforcement learning on a simulated Indian grocery shop. For most of it, nothing worked. This is what we got wrong, and how a 4B model got to 89% of a scripted shopkeeper over a week.

The Karobaar Gym team·Draft, November 2026·18 min read

Every neighbourhood in India has a kirana shop. The owner decides what to stock and how much, haggles over prices, keeps a notebook of who owes what, chases suppliers and files GST. None of it is exotic. It is also exactly the kind of work people say AI agents will soon do for small businesses.

So we built one. KiranaBench is a simulated kirana shop in which a language model plays four people at once: the manager, the cashier, the helper and the munshi, who keeps the books. It runs the shop for 30 simulated days. We gave it to four open models. One of them made money, and a scripted shopkeeper we wrote ourselves made more than seven times as much.

Since then we have been trying to close that gap by training a small model, Qwen3.5-4B, with reinforcement learning. For most of the month it didn't work. In the last few days it started to: the model now runs the shop for a week at 89% of the scripted shopkeeper, by learning from that shopkeeper before learning from the reward. This post covers how we set the problem up, what went wrong, how we got there, and what we would tell anyone building an RL environment for a long, messy task. Most of what went wrong was in our environment and our reward, not in the model.

The shop

RL needs a score that can be computed rather than judged. A shop is good for this. At closing time the cash, the stock and the khata (the credit book) add up to a net worth, and it either went up or it didn't.

The simulator owns every number. A second model plays the customers, who bargain in English, Hindi or Hinglish, and the suppliers, who answer orders by email. Neither can change a price or a quantity. Those come from fixed rules, seeded per shop, so the same seed always gives the same shop. The model we're testing sees the business only the way its staff would:

  1. messages from customers, suppliers and the other staff;
  2. check_books, which shows cash, stock, khata and what sold yesterday;
  3. a few tools to set prices, and email, which is how orders reach suppliers.

Running costs are ₹500 a day, so a shop where nobody does anything loses about ₹1.1 lakh over the month. That's our floor. The ceiling is the scripted owner, a hand-written policy that reorders from the last 30 days of sales. Every result in this post sits somewhere between the two.

One more detail matters later. Suppliers get orders as free-text emails, written the way a shopkeeper would write them. A third model (Mistral Small 3.2) reads each email and turns it into line items for the simulator.

Benchmark first

Before training anything, we ran the benchmark properly: four models, three shops each, 30 days per shop. Then we read all 360 simulated days in full. In an environment like this, a number is only as good as the logs behind it. Our audits found real simulator bugs more than once. In one, the staff never answered the manager. In another, an undefined price field let items sell below cost. Neither bug produced an error. Both produced plausible numbers.

The models fail in ways a shopkeeper would recognise. In an early 60-day shakedown, llama3.1:8b ordered stock in the first few days, then got stuck writing instructions to its staff and never restocked. From day 31 it sold nothing.

Picking a model and a trainer

We chose Qwen3.5-4B. In a probe against the 9B version it was about as capable, gave more training signal and cost half as much to run. Untrained, it does badly. Over 30 days on five fresh shops (seeds 20–24) it lost ₹1,33,382 on average, more than the shop nobody runs, and beat that idle shop on only 2 of the 5.

For training we use OpenPipe's ART library with GRPO. The idea is simple: play the same situation several times, score every attempt, and push the model towards the attempts that scored better. We don't train on whole months. An episode is one saved day from a real run. The model plays the day, and we score what happened.

Choosing the reward

The obvious reward is the day's change in net worth. It's simple and nobody can argue with it. It's also wrong in one specific way: harm that shows up later is invisible.

Our red-team pass found the clearest case. One attempt over-ordered ₹2.8 lakh of stock. Goods on order still count towards net worth, so the day itself barely moved, and the day-only reward scored the blunder at −₹2,094. The real cost of that order lands in the days after.

The reward we settled on, which we call B3, is the day's change in net worth plus three more days played by the scripted owner. The scripted days bring delayed damage into the score. To pick the number of days, we checked which reward best agreed with how the same decisions turned out when the model kept playing. Three scripted days agreed best (0.78), ahead of the day alone (0.69) and ten scripted days (0.62).

Remember the scripted owner who plays those three days. It comes back.

The parser that lost ₹15.9 lakh

Early on, a regular expression stood in for the email parser. On seed 20 it misread enough orders to book a ₹15.9 lakh loss. Every run before the fix was invalid, including our first baselines, and we reran them with the model-based parser from the benchmark.

On 1 November it happened again on a smaller scale. A model wrote an order as atta_5kg 10x280, meaning 10 packs at ₹280 each. The parser read every line in that email as 280 packs, no price, and booked ₹5.2 lakh of goods with one supplier. One such day moves the average over our 12 held-out situations by about ₹30,000. The fix was a single sentence in the parser's instructions. (10 @ 280 was always read correctly, if you ever need to email a wholesaler.)

The rest of that day's ₹7.1 lakh loss was the model's own doing. It really did order 600 bags of 10 kg atta, and it sent the same order eight times to six suppliers.

The parser is part of the environment. Anything that turns the model's words into simulator actions can produce a large, plausible, wrong reward. Test it on the exact text your model writes, not on the text you expect it to write.

Run 1: the model learned to do less

Our first real run was 20 steps of GRPO. The held-out score barely moved:

Then we ran the trained model for 30 full days on the same five shops as before. It lost ₹92,677, against ₹1,33,382 untrained, and it beat the idle shop on 4 of 5 shops instead of 2. That looks like progress. With five shops it isn't significant (t = 0.80).

When we read the days, the reason was clear: the model had learned to do less. A few training days had disastrous outcomes, like the giant orders above, with huge negative rewards. The cheapest way to avoid those is to stop ordering. We started calling this catastrophe-driven risk aversion.

Run 2, and the engine that died quietly

Run 2 was bigger: 45 steps of 64 rollouts each, in two copies with different settings. Every held-out eval came in below step 0. One copy was stopped at step 25 by an entropy guard we had added to catch the model collapsing onto the same few outputs. We paused the other at step 37.

Along the way, the vLLM engine inside the trainer kept dying or hanging. Worse, some eval days were scored after it had died. Every model call failed, the shop sat untouched, and the simulator faithfully scored an idle day. Two of our eval points turned out to be invalid. Now a day with failed calls and no working engine is never scored, and a watchdog restarts any run whose engine stops.

The teacher was doing the homework

The real problem took longer to find than it should have.

In training, the model didn't play the whole day. It played the morning handover, from 09:40 to 11:30, and the scripted owner ran the rest of the day before the three bootstrap days. Whatever the model forgot to order, the scripted owner ordered for it, first that afternoon and again in the bootstrap days. So doing nothing scored almost as well as ordering well, and the gradient had very little to say about the one decision we cared about most.

The same split caused a second problem. Training only ever covered the morning, but the eval is the whole day. The afternoon was never trained, and it drifted: by one checkpoint the model was calling check_books about 80 times a day.

Check what your bootstrap policy can undo. A scripted continuation is a good way to bring delayed consequences into the reward. It's a bad one if it also fixes the model's mistakes before they cost anything.

Copying the owner first

Our next run fixed both problems: training windows started at different points in the day, and after the model's turns the shop ran idle instead of being rescued. But each training step took about three hours, so 30 steps would have taken four days, and RL was still being asked to find good ordering in a model that almost never ordered well. We stopped it early and asked a smaller question first: can the model at least copy the owner?

That is behaviour cloning. We fine-tuned the base model on 825 examples of the scripted owner's own decisions and compared the result (bc1) with the base model and the owner on 12 held-out situations (two attempts per situation for each model).

bc1 copies the owner's habits. Its days are short, it places orders and goes home, and it makes no failed tool calls. It doesn't copy the decisions. Its average day profit is still negative (−₹1,132, against the owner's ₹4,847), and in one situation it ordered 111 bags of atta and no milk at all.

Our guess, which we haven't tested yet, is that we're asking it to copy numbers it can't see. The owner sizes orders from 30-day sales rates. check_books shows the model stock and yesterday's sales, not those rates. So we changed two things.

Showing the model what the owner sees

The first change was to the shop, not the model. A real owner knows roughly how fast each item sells; the billing machine has the last month. So the books now show, for every item, what customers asked for a day over the last 30 days: what sold, plus what they asked for and didn't find, so the number doesn't collapse after a stockout. On an empty shop the old books said "sold yesterday 0" for everything, and the model had nothing to order from. This alone moved the untrained model's day from −₹1,347 to −₹733.

Copying the teacher's arithmetic

The second change was to the training examples. bc1 copied the owner's tool calls. bc2 copies the owner's reasoning too, written in the numbers the model can now see, in the place where the model does its own thinking:

Metro Bulk Store is the cheapest (cash & carry), paid in advance. One order of every dry pack below 2.5 days of stock, topped up to 4 days:
- atta_1kg: customers ask for 81.3/day; have 11 = 0.1 days. 4 days is 325, less 11: 314 packs.
- chai_250g: customers ask for 18.2/day; have 8 = 0.4 days. 4 days is 73, less 8: 65 packs.

On days from two shops it never trained on, bc2 wrote 15 of 40 decisions token for token as the owner would (bc1: 9), reasoning and quantities included. Played for real, it was uneven. On some shops it came within ₹1,200 of the owner's day. On others it did things the owner never does: on one day it put milk into the cash-and-carry order, 2,436 packs of 500 ml and 684 of 1 litre. The fridge couldn't hold it, and ₹89,385 of milk was paid for and lost at the door. On 3 of 24 days it checked the books and went home without ordering anything. Its mean day was −₹3,603. Training on 3.6 times as much of the owner's data (bc3) didn't fix this: −₹1,673.

Correcting its own mistakes

A model trained only on the owner's days has never seen the owner recover from the model's mistakes. DAgger, an old idea from imitation learning, fixes exactly that. The model plays; at every state it reaches, the owner says what it would do there, and why; the model is trained on those corrections, and plays again. The owner's private memory is put back after each answer, so it labels the shop as it actually is: if the model skipped the milk, the next label orders it.

A second round reached the owner's day profit (₹4,831 against ₹4,847) and held up on 12 shops from new seeds: on 11 of them it was within a few percent of the owner.

A week on its own

Then we let it run the shop alone for seven days, with its own memory, instead of one day followed by the scripted owner. It made 45% of what the owner made. Day 1 matched; by day 6 it was losing money. The reason was the same one as before, one horizon further out: every correction so far had come from a model's first day, so nobody had ever told it what to do in the shop its own week had made.

The third round collected the corrections from week-long runs:

It still drifts, later. In a second week alone it holds 57% of the owner (first 12 runs), because no correction yet comes from beyond day 7. Each round has pushed the drift further out, which is encouraging and also a warning: if every extra week needs its own round, imitation alone won't get us to a month.

RL from the taught model, too early

Once round 1 matched the owner on a day, we tried going past it with GRPO, starting from the taught model and rewarding the day's profit. In three steps it fell from ₹4,329 a day to ₹787, with one day at −₹86,846. The model's sampling entropy fell from 0.081 to 0.034 and a guard stopped the run. Starting from a good model is not enough on its own; the update has to be kept close to what the model was taught, and our KL term pointed at the base model instead. We will come back to RL once the week is solid.

What we'd tell you

  • Read the days, not just the score. Every serious bug we found produced plausible numbers, not errors. We only found them by reading logs.
  • Know your floor and ceiling before you train. A shop nobody runs and a scripted owner told us what "better" meant. Without them, −₹92,677 would have looked like a result.
  • Treat the parser as part of the environment. It cost us ₹15.9 lakh once and ₹5.2 lakh again.
  • Never score a day the model didn't play. A dead inference engine looks exactly like a cautious shopkeeper.
  • Check what your continuation policy can undo. Ours erased the training signal for ordering.
  • Report the median next to the mean. One runaway day moved our 12-situation mean by about ₹30,000.
  • Show the model what your teacher sees. Our scripted owner ordered from 30-day rates the model's books didn't show. Copying it was asking for numbers out of thin air.
  • Correct the model where it actually goes. More of the teacher's own days made things worse; corrections on the model's own days, and then its own weeks, fixed them.
  • Don't let RL loose on a taught model without an anchor. Three steps undid what imitation had built.

What's next

A fourth round of corrections, on twice as many weeks, is training now; after it, corrections from two-week runs, for the second-week drop. Then RL again, this time kept close to the taught model, to see if it can beat the owner rather than copy it. We also want to test the environment itself: a policy that tries to exploit the rules, and an upper bound on profit, so a score can be read as a share of the best possible. The kirana shop is the first of several businesses in Karobaar Gym. The truck fleet simulator is built and gets its first model runs next. A municipal corporation, a wedding planner and a staffing agency are in design, and a kabaadi wala, a hotel and a cloud kitchen were started at the end of October.
